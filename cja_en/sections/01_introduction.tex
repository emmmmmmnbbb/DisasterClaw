\section{Introduction}
An aerial inspection agent may locate a damaged area and still lack the evidence needed to answer a specific question about it. A wide view supports regional coverage, whereas a closer view allocates more image pixels to a smaller area. Moving the sensor therefore changes both the available detail and the context retained for interpretation. In disaster inspection, this trade-off matters because questions about individual buildings, damage counts, and the spatial distribution of severe damage require different evidence. An additional observation is useful only when its contribution to the requested decision justifies the observation cost.

Aerial vision-and-language navigation has developed datasets and agents for following instructions through outdoor environments. AerialVLN and CityNav establish substantial navigation settings, while OpenFly provides a platform with trajectories spanning multiple heights and scenes~\cite{liu2023aerialvln,lee2025citynav,gao2026openfly}. These developments make motion-conditioned visual reasoning a concrete research problem. Navigation success, however, does not directly measure whether a new view improves a disaster assessment. Building-damage data such as xBD~\cite{gupta2019xbd} support evaluation of visual classification, but a change in building-level accuracy alone does not reveal which inspection questions benefit from an agent's sensing decisions.

Foundation models provide a practical interface between task language, visual evidence, and tools. Modular navigation systems, hierarchical semantic planning, and aerial agent frameworks already combine these components~\cite{shah2023lmnav,zhang2025citynavagent,li2026aerialclaw}. CityEQA connects active urban exploration to question answering~\cite{zhao2025cityeqa}, while disaster interpretation agents perform sequential analysis of remote-sensing images~\cite{liu2024rescueadi}. Their capabilities motivate a narrower question here: when observations are limited, do uncertainty-based reobservation rules direct the sensor toward evidence that changes task outcomes? This question cannot be answered by the existence of an action--observation loop or by an increase in a model's self-reported confidence. It requires paired outcomes and an account of where the observation budget was spent.

We address this question using DisasterClaw, a single-UAV inspection implementation operating over georeferenced disaster imagery. The study separates a controlled building-level experiment from an online question-answering experiment. In the first, each building has paired predictions at three camera footprints, allowing observation selection to be compared under a common building budget. In the second, the agent receives a rendered image and structured evidence, can request additional observations, and returns an answer or abstention. This separation is necessary: the offline allocator ranks a collection of buildings, whereas the online controller decides whether to inspect again within one question. They use related uncertainty signals but solve different allocation problems.

The contributions are threefold. First, we specify an imagery-based observation model and an evaluation design that separates changes in visual classification from changes in inspection answers. Second, we reconstruct budget-dependent performance from paired predictions and distinguish beneficial from harmful reobservations, with exploratory uncertainty estimates that retain within-region dependence. Third, we trace online observation use to question types and outcomes, identifying a concrete concentration of actions on already-correct cases. These are mechanism and evaluation contributions within the recorded setting. The available experiments do not support a new optimal controller, a state-of-the-art navigation claim, or generalization to disasters unseen by the perception backbone.
